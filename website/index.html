One curve per dated activity

The Gantt header adds every eligible activity's daily contribution at each point. Its weight is the total effort across the entire curve, so the daily peak changes with the activity's duration. The effort badge shows that weight before the duration badge.

Deadline pressure grows slowly and peaks at the end. Planned pace follows a bell-shaped daily curve derived from cumulative S-curve progress. Both include a short exponential tail after the end. Choose a model with the gear button; the choice stays in this browser.

The vertical scale stays at 0–4 daily units: green through 1, yellow at 2 and red from 3. Sums above 4 are still calculated, but the graph clips them visually.

The challenge of distributing classroom work over time

When an assignment opens, two well-known ideas help describe how its workload may unfold: Parkinson's Law (Parkinson, 1957) describes work expanding to fill the available time; the Student Syndrome (Goldratt, 1997) describes postponing substantial work until the deadline feels urgent. Together, they motivate the model in which daily effort rises slowly at first, then sharply near the deadline. The planned model spreads daily effort into a bell shape, corresponding to an S-shaped curve of cumulative progress. Both models include a short exponential decline after the deadline.

Research links distant deadlines with a greater tendency to procrastinate (Steel, 2007). A longitudinal study of students associated procrastination with greater stress later in the term and lower grades (Tice and Baumeister, 1997). The exponential rise shown here is an illustrative model; it does not imply that every student concentrates work in the final 48 hours.

Partial, sequential deliverables can help distribute effort. Intermediate deadlines have been shown to reduce procrastination and improve performance (Ariely and Wertenbroch, 2002), while spaced practice supports longer-term retention (Cepeda et al., 2006).
